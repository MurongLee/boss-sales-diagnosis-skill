# Diagnosis Framework

Use this as the core judgment loop. The output should feel like deal review, not classroom instruction.

## 1. Symptom vs Real Blockage

Start by separating what the user sees from what may actually be happening.

Common symptoms:
- customer says price is high
- proposal sent, no reply
- contact says the boss is busy
- customer keeps asking for more material
- sales team says "the customer is interested"
- competitor offers a lower price
- internal boss refuses resources

Possible real blockages:
- the customer does not see business impact
- the contact lacks internal influence
- the real decision-maker has not been reached
- the solution answers product needs, not business anxiety
- the seller is comparing products while the customer is judging risk
- trust is based on friendliness, not capability
- the deal has low strategic value and should not consume more resources

## 2. Four Needs Map

Diagnose whether the solution covers all four demand layers:

1. **Customer explicit need**: what the organization says it wants.
2. **Customer hidden need**: what the organization needs but has not clearly articulated.
3. **Decision-maker explicit need**: what the responsible person must justify internally.
4. **Decision-maker hidden need**: personal safety, credit, reduced blame, career upside, reliable partner.

A strong solution is not a product description. It is an answer sheet for these four layers.

## 3. Trust Type

Classify the missing or existing trust.

- **Capability trust**: "Can you solve this problem?"
- **Character trust**: "Will you tell the truth and keep your promises?"
- **Process trust**: "Will cooperation with you be stable and low-friction?"
- **Personal-risk trust**: "Will choosing you make me safer, not more exposed?"

Do not confuse personal friendliness with business trust. A customer may like the seller and still not trust the seller with the result.

## 4. Value Translation

Check whether the seller translated value into the customer's world.

Weak translation:
- feature list
- product advantage
- "we are professional"
- "our service is good"
- "our price is reasonable"

Strong translation:
- cost of doing nothing
- risk avoided
- efficiency gained
- decision-maker can explain internally
- implementation path is believable
- long-term benefit is larger than short-term price difference

## 5. Owner's Investment Judgment

For owner/operator users, always judge whether the deal deserves more investment.

Assess:
- deal amount and gross margin
- long-term customer value
- reference value
- strategic entry value
- probability of becoming a bad customer
- whether extra resources create durable advantage or merely buy one order

Possible decisions:
- **Invest**: worth owner attention and customized solution.
- **Repair**: worth one more high-quality attempt after reframing.
- **Nurture**: stop pushing; maintain low-frequency high-value contact.
- **Stop**: opportunity quality is too poor or values conflict.

## 6. Max-Win-Win Path

A good recommendation should improve the outcome for more than one side.

Ask:
- How does the customer win beyond paying less?
- How does the decision-maker become safer or more successful?
- How does the seller avoid unprofitable overcommitment?
- How does the company build reusable trust, capability, or case value?

If the answer only helps the seller close, it is not enough.

## 7. Action Design

Every diagnosis should end with action.

Good actions are:
- specific
- sequenced
- low in ambiguity
- respectful of customer time
- designed to reveal truth
- able to create or restore trust

Bad actions:
- "follow up more"
- "build relationship"
- "explain value"
- "offer discount"
- "send more information"

Rewrite bad actions into concrete moves:
- send one high-quality reframe message
- ask one risk-revealing question
- prepare one-page ROI/risk note
- request a 15-minute alignment call
- stop chasing and switch to long-term nurturing
